The Doctor in the Dark

Dr. McKee was a physician in Savoy on the night the cyclone struck. What he saw, and what he did in the hours after, tells you something about who you needed to be to live in a frontier town in 1880.

Home › Tales from Savoy › The Doctor in the Dark
Era May 28, 1880 — The Great Cyclone

There was no warning system. No telegraph could outrun the storm; the storm destroyed the telegraph lines when it arrived. There was no siren, no radio, no organized civil defense. There was the sound from the west, and then there was the darkness and the roaring, and then there was the aftermath.

Dr. McKee was a physician in Savoy. His wife broke her arm in the storm. That single fact — found in the newspaper accounts filed the next day — contains an entire night inside it. Somewhere in those five seconds, in the collapse or the chaos that followed, Mrs. McKee's arm broke. And Dr. McKee, whose own house may or may not have been standing, was a physician in a town with fifty or sixty injured people and no functioning hospital.

The seminary — the Academy, the college building — became the hospital. It was one of the few structures left standing, and the people who organized the response in those first hours understood immediately what it had to become. Bedding was hauled out of the debris. Makeshift beds were improvised on the floors. Guards were posted at the doors to keep out the curious and the unhelpful, because a crowd always comes, and a crowd is not the same as help.

Dr. McKee worked through the night. That is an inference, not a direct quotation, but it is the only inference available when you have fifty or sixty injured people and one of the town's physicians with a broken-armed wife and a ruined town. The doctor from Paris, Texas — a Dr. Stell — arrived on the first relief train from the east, as did other physicians from Sherman and Denison. By morning there were enough hands.

But in those first hours, in the dark before the relief trains arrived, it was the people already there who had to manage. Dr. McKee was one of those people. He had come to Savoy, as settlers came to Savoy, because there was a railroad and a college and a growing town and what looked like a future. He had not come to be a disaster physician. He became one anyway, as frontier settlers routinely became things they had not planned to be, because the frontier did not offer the option of declining.

Mrs. McKee broke her arm. Dr. Stell came from Paris on the first relief train. The seminary was converted to a hospital within hours. — accounts from the North Texas Express, May 29–30, 1880

By daylight, relief engines were running in from Bonham, Sherman, Denison, Bells, and Dodd City. They brought doctors and nurses and food and ice and medicine, which tells you something about what Savoy needed most in the morning: not just medicine, but ice, for the July heat that would come, for the fevers that would follow the injuries. Someone thought ahead. Someone in Bonham or Sherman, told at one in the morning that Savoy had been destroyed, thought: send ice.

Mrs. McKee's arm healed. The town rebuilt. Dr. McKee's name appears in the archive in only this one moment — the night the cyclone came, the broken arm, the darkness — and then he passes back into the ordinary run of frontier medical practice, which was its own kind of heroism, unreported.
