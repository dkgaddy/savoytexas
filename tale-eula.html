Eula Phillips, Stringer

For years, Eula Phillips filed news dispatches from Savoy to the county paper. She wasn't paid much. She was paid attention. The whole town knew that if something happened in Savoy, Eula would write it down.

Home › Tales from Savoy › Eula Phillips, Stringer
Era Mid-20th Century — Community

In small-town journalism, the stringer is a particular figure. Not an editor, not a reporter in the full sense — more like the community's designated observer, the person who calls in the items: the church socials, the visiting relatives, the school honors, the club meetings, the deaths. The stringer is not paid much, usually. What they are paid is attention. The whole town knows they are writing it down.

Eula Phillips was Savoy's stringer. For years — the archive's 2007 profile suggests decades — she was the person who filed the Savoy items to the county paper. Not the big news, necessarily; that came through other channels. The texture of daily life. Who had come to visit, who had been to the hospital, who had won the spelling contest at school. The kind of reporting that seems trivial until fifty years have passed and a historian is trying to understand what it was like to live in a place, and finds that the stringer's items are the only record that bothered to notice.

She was a character. That word gets used loosely, but in Eula Phillips's case it is earned: the 2007 profile describes a woman with opinions and a sharp eye and the particular confidence of someone who has been the town's designated correspondent long enough to know she serves a function no one else is filling. She had her ways of doing things. She had her sources. She had her sense of what mattered in Savoy and what did not.

Eula Phillips filed the Savoy items to the county paper for decades — church socials, visiting relatives, school honors, deaths. In 2007, the paper wrote a profile of her. She had been paying attention to Savoy for longer than most people could remember. — Bonham Daily Favorite, 2007

The 2007 profile appeared in the Bonham Daily Favorite, the county paper she had served for so long. The fact that the paper turned the attention back on her — profiled the correspondent rather than just running her dispatches — is its own acknowledgment of what she had given them. Savoy was not a big dateline. Savoy was a small town on Highway 82 in Fannin County, with a population that had been declining for decades and a history that was mostly in the past. But someone in Savoy was watching, and writing it down, and calling it in.

This is one of the least celebrated but most important things any community does for itself: it keeps a record. Not the formal archive kind, not the historian's kind — the newspaper-items kind, the accumulation of ordinary detail that becomes, over decades, the texture of what a place was like when it was alive. Eula Phillips gave Savoy that. The county paper, by writing about her in 2007, gave her her due.
