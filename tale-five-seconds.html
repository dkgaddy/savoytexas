Five Seconds

The hotel keeper was asked afterward what it was like. He thought about it and said: five seconds. That was all the time the cyclone gave the town of Savoy before it was gone.

Home › Tales from Savoy › Five Seconds
Era May 28, 1880 — The Great Cyclone

The hotel keeper was one of the first people interviewed by the correspondent who arrived on the overnight train from Bonham. The reporter had been sent in a hurry, on the first available engine, when word of the disaster reached the city at one o'clock in the morning. He found Savoy in ruins.

What did it feel like, the reporter asked, when it hit?

Five seconds, the hotel keeper said. He had been awake, or nearly so — it was after ten o'clock at night on a Friday — when the sound came from the west. He knew what a train sounded like; this was a railroad town. But this train never reached the station. By the time he understood what it was, it was already over.

Five seconds. Forty houses demolished. Every business in town destroyed but one. Nine people dead, fifty or sixty injured. The hotel itself — the account does not say precisely what became of it, whether it stood or fell — reduced to something that required the word "ruins" in every sentence the reporter filed.

The tornado was roughly 178 yards wide. It tracked through the center of town, following a path that a later generation would mark on maps as if measurement could make sense of it. The damage zone was absolute — within the funnel's path, almost nothing was left. At the edges, the wind played its familiar cruel game: one house standing, the next one matchsticks, no logic apparent to the people who picked through it the next morning.

The hotel keeper was not a man given to elaborate description. He was a man who ran a hotel on a railroad line in North Texas in 1880, which is to say a practical man, accustomed to the frontier's ordinary violence of weather and accident and hard luck. When the reporter asked him what it was like, he did not say that the sky turned green or that the air smelled of sulphur or that he heard the roar of a freight train, as the later disaster vocabulary would have it. He said five seconds. And then he had to go see about his building.

"Five seconds." — the Savoy hotel keeper, interviewed the morning of May 29, 1880, by a correspondent for the North Texas Express

The reporters who came by train that night and the next morning — from Bonham, Sherman, Denison, eventually from papers as far as the Brenham Weekly Banner — wrote long dispatches full of the names of the dead and the injured and the strange facts a tornado leaves behind. One man found three hundred yards from where he had been standing. A family intact in a house that had been moved four feet off its foundation. The post office, swept clean.

But the hotel keeper's five seconds is the one that stays with you. Not because it is dramatic, but because it is not. It is the measurement a practical man makes in the immediate aftermath, before the grief sets in, while he is still in the part of the disaster where you are simply trying to understand what happened. Five seconds. And then the rest of your life in that town, whatever it becomes.
