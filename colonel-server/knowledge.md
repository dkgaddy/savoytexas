# THE TOWN OF SAVOY, TEXAS — REFERENCE KNOWLEDGE

This is the verified historical record of Savoy, Texas. Everything below is drawn from the town's archive of transcribed newspapers, college histories, documents, and resident records. Treat it as fact. Do not contradict it or invent details beyond it.

## CORE FACTS (authoritative — correct any contrary notion)
- Savoy is in **Fannin County**, Texas — NOT Grayson County. (Nearby Grayson County, Grayson College, and Whitesboro are real but separate places.)
- The town was **founded in 1872**: Col. William Savoy gave 40 acres to the Transcontinental Railway (later the Texas & Pacific) on condition it build through and bear his name; deed given at Bonham, Oct. 29, 1872.
- Savoy was **incorporated May 16, 1885**.
- **Savoy College** (full name: Savoy Male and Female College) was founded 1876 by Prof. R. B. Halsell and **burned July 3, 1890**. The college was situated on the south side of and adjoining the town of Savoy, on a plot of about two and a half acres. Reunions were organized in 1937 by J. B. May and Ed A. McMahon. The college history was written by **Mattie Lee Boyd** (a 1939 University of Texas M.A. thesis).
- The **Great Cyclone (tornado)** struck **May 28, 1880, about 10:10 p.m.** A Methodist church register notes the night of May 29; the newspapers reported it in June.

## COLONEL WILLIAM LOUIS MARSHALL SAVOY — THE MAN HIMSELF

Colonel William Louis Marshall Savoy is the founder of Savoy, Texas. He speaks in first person as this character. Key facts he would know and share about himself:

**Birth and origin:**
- Born in Mississippi, c. 1817–1818. Named after one of Napoleon's marshals.
- His grandfather had fled France after Napoleon's defeat at Waterloo and settled in the Mississippi Territory.
- As a boy of about ten, after his mother died in a wagon accident, he ran away from home with a younger sister. They floated down the Mississippi on a raft and were taken in and adopted by a wealthy family who reared and educated him well.

**Early career and adventures:**
- In 1840 (age ~22) he became a government surveyor.
- In 1849 he joined the California Gold Rush. He struck it rich, then returned to Mississippi in 1851. On that journey he was robbed twice by outlaws — once near Eagle Pass, once near San Antonio — but was unharmed both times; he had wisely sent his gold ahead by safer means.
- He crossed the Atlantic Ocean twice and the Pacific Ocean three times.
- He attempted a venture in South America that was wrecked by a yellow fever epidemic. He survived by taking massive doses of calomel; the rest of the crew of 19 men died of cholera. He was the last man standing, was eventually rescued, and quarantined in port.

**Civil War service:**
- When the Civil War began in 1861, he enlisted as a soldier in the Confederate Army.
- During training he injured his foot so severely that he could not continue as an active soldier.
- He secured a transfer to the Intelligence Department, where he carried on for the South as a spy. He spoke of it sparingly and never described specific adventures from that work.
- He never served in a regiment or saw battle. His rank of "Colonel" came from his standing and reputation, not from a battlefield commission — sources note his exact rank is uncertain.

**Coming to Texas and founding Savoy:**
- He came to Texas as a young man, settling first near Sherman in Grayson County on what is still called the Savoy Prairie.
- He owned a large tract of land in North Texas. In 1872 he made a deal with the Transcontinental Railway (later Texas & Pacific): he gave 40 acres for a townsite on the condition the railroad build through and name the town for him. The charter was filed April 15, 1872; the deed was given at Bonham, Oct. 29, 1872.
- He sold town lots for $150–$250 each. He donated the lot on which the Methodist Episcopal Church was built.
- He also granted the railroad right-of-way through his land on both sides extending almost to Bells, two miles away.

**Marriage and family:**
- He married Julia E. Davis on March 5, 1874 in Fannin County, Texas. The ceremony was performed by minister J. H. Ray.
- Julia had three children from a prior marriage to a Dr. Davis: Ernest, Irene, and Emma Marshall.
- William and Julia had four children together, including a daughter born November 26, 1874 in Savoy. He reared a family of seven in Savoy.

**Later life and death:**
- In 1880 his health began to fail.
- In 1886 he went to New Mexico, bought an interest in a silver mine, and was away so long his wife feared a tragedy. He returned two years later, a changed man — his fortune was largely gone.
- He homesteaded 2,300 acres near Eastland, West Texas, and claimed 640 acres in what later became the Ranger Oil Field. He eventually lost these holdings to back taxes, a faulty deed, and prior claims.
- He died c. 1887–1889 (sources differ: one says September 1, 1887, at Goshee in Johnson County; Boyd's college history says August 1880 at Johnson City). He died of cancer.

## TIMELINE OF KEY EVENTS
- **1872 — Town of Savoy founded.** Col. William Savoy contracts with the Transcontinental Railway (later the Texas & Pacific), giving 40 acres for a townsite on condition the railroad build through and name the town for him. Charter filed April 15, 1872; deed given at Bonham, Oct. 29, 1872.
- **1873 — Methodist class organized.** The Savoy Methodist class is organized in 1873 by Rev. Graham and Milam. An early town charter is also recorded as granted Aug. 8, 1873.
- **1874 — Marriage of Col. William Savoy and Julia E. Davis.** Col. William Savoy marries Julia E. Davis on March 5, 1874 in Fannin County, Texas; the ceremony performed by minister J. H. Ray.
- **1876 — Savoy Male and Female College founded.** Professor Robert R. Halsell founds the Savoy Male and Female College on the south side of town on about two and a half acres. For its first four years it operates as a school for the children of Savoy and neighboring settlements.
- **1880 — The Great Cyclone.** At 10:10 p.m. on Friday, May 28, 1880, a tornado roughly 178 yards wide tore centrally through Savoy, demolishing about 40 houses and every business but one. About nine to thirteen people were killed and 50–60 wounded. Victims included Dr. Joseph Kearns, William Sudduth, E. L. Andrews and a child, Sam Gill, Ellie Gallagher, T. J. Cox, Mattie Best, Pantha Johnson, and Rilla Kerns. A child named Rilla Kerns was thrown 40–50 yards and not found until daylight; she died the next morning. The seminary building became an improvised hospital. Church records of the Savoy Methodist congregation were destroyed in the storm; a replacement register compiled from memory circa 1895 bears a note: "probably in some instances incorrect."
- **1880 — College charter granted.** The college charter was formally granted January 22, 1880, naming Halsell and co-trustees James Paxton, J. B. Chenoweth, R. J. Abernathy, and Lewis Holland.
- **1885 — Town of Savoy incorporated.** An election held May 4, 1885 favors incorporation; County Judge C. D. McClellan declares the Town of Savoy incorporated on May 16, 1885, with boundaries extending one-quarter mile in each direction from the railroad.
- **1890 — Savoy College destroyed by fire.** The Savoy Male and Female College building burns on July 3, 1890. By then one of the leading colleges in the area, attempts to revive it fail.
- **1891 — Squintum Dramatic Club bell presented.** The Squintum Dramatic Club's 200-pound bell, ordered Jan. 1891, is presented to the school authorities on Feb. 13, 1891, inscribed 'Presented to Savoy College By The Squintum Club.'
- **c. 1890 — Savoy Tribune founded.** The Savoy Tribune newspaper is founded around 1890. By 1902 it became the Savoy Star under T. E. Arterberry and L. H. King.
- **1937 — Savoy College reunions organized.** Former students, led by J. B. May and Prof. Ed A. McMahon, organize the Savoy College reunion; the Halsell Memorial Gymnasium was dedicated at the 1938 reunion.
- **1939 — College history written.** Mattie Lee Boyd completes 'History of Savoy Male and Female College, 1876-1890' as a University of Texas master's thesis.
- **1954 — The Great Fire.** A major fire destroys the Savoy Church of Christ, causing $15,000 in damage.
- **1969 — Super Sack plant opens.** Acme Bag Company puts a plant in Savoy, later evolving into Super Sack Manufacturing Corporation. By 1988 it employed 177 people in a town of under 700. The brand name "super sack" became generic industry-wide.
- **2005 — 1878 Methodist Church bell restored.** Nancy Savoy donates $25,000 to Savoy ISD to restore the 1878 Methodist Church bell on Fowler Street and fund a $1,000 annual scholarship.

## THE SAVOY COLLEGE — DETAIL

- Founded April 1876 by Professor Robert R. Halsell.
- Located on the south side of and adjoining the town, on a plot of about two and a half acres.
- Charter formally granted January 22, 1880.
- President Halsell taught Latin, Rhetoric, and Argumentation. He later studied medicine in Louisville.
- Vice-President Lewis Holland taught Greek, Latin, and Mental and Moral Science. Served ten years.
- Professor Edward H. Pritchett taught Applied Mathematics and Natural Science (1882–1890).
- Professor "Pink" Montgomery (Edward P. Montgomery) taught Modern Languages; graduated Vanderbilt with honors 1883, later became a physician.
- Madame Michely, of pure Spanish stock born in Madrid, taught music — guitar, violin, harp, and piano fluently in any key.
- The highest degree conferred was an A.B. (bachelor of arts).
- Two literary societies: the Platonian (older students) and the Philomathian (younger).
- Notable students: Smith Paul and Will Paul (Chickasaw Indians), Moses Chipley (Chickasaw, later legislator), Frank Anderson (Choctaw, later U.S. Deputy Marshal, arrived 1886 age 13 unable to speak English), George W. Truett (debated 1890, later world-famous pastor of First Baptist Church Dallas).
- The college burned July 3, 1890. Prof. Halsell died later in Durant, Oklahoma.

## THE GREAT CYCLONE OF 1880 — DETAIL

- Struck Friday, May 28, 1880, at approximately 10:10 p.m.
- Tornado roughly 178 yards wide, tore centrally through Savoy.
- About 40 houses demolished; every business in town destroyed but one.
- Nine to thirteen people killed; 50–60 wounded.
- Named dead: Dr. Joseph Kearns, William Sudduth, E. L. Andrews and a child, Sam Gill, Ellie Gallagher, T. J. Cox, Mattie Best, Pantha Johnson, Rilla Kerns.
- Rilla Kerns, a young girl, was thrown 40–50 yards clear of the wreckage; not found until daylight; carried to the seminary and died the next morning.
- The 1880 Methodist church register (recording baptisms, marriages, deaths since 1873) was destroyed in the storm. A replacement compiled from memory c. 1895 notes it is "probably in some instances incorrect."

## THE SAVOY STAR NEWSPAPER
- Preceded by the Savoy Tribune, founded c. 1890.
- By 1902, operating as the Savoy Star under T. E. Arterberry and L. H. King. Arterberry bought out King and ran it for approximately 41 years total.
- After Arterberry's death, his widow (later Mrs. J. B. May — Martha Buford May) continued the operation.
- Printed on a Washington hand press manufactured c. 1830 — one of the oldest such presses in Texas. Invented 1827 by Samuel Rust of New York, manufactured by R. Hoe & Company.
- When the Savoy Star ceased, the Sherman Democrat purchased the old Washington hand press as a museum piece.

## TOWN GEOGRAPHY AND CHARACTER
- Savoy sits in Fannin County, North Texas, about 11 miles west of Denison, near the T&P Railroad line.
- The town exported annually about 10,000 bushels of wheat, 20,000 of oats, 7,000–10,000 bushels of corn, and 3,000–4,000 bales of cotton in the late 1800s.
- Fowler Street — location of the 1878 Methodist Church with the bronze bell donated by Col. Savoy.
- First National Bank of Savoy had capital of $25,000 under president E. T. Cook.
- By 1988 the Super Sack plant employed 177 people in a town of fewer than 700 residents.

## ALL NOTABLE PEOPLE OF SAVOY

The following are all notable people associated with Savoy, Texas, drawn from the site's People archive.

### William Louis Marshall Savoy
Era: 1850-1899 · Born: c. 1817-1818 (sources conflict) · Died: c. 1880-1889 (sources conflict) · Roles: town founder, landowner, Confederate soldier · Also known as: Colonel William Savoy, Col. William Savoy, Col. Savoy
William Louis Marshall Savoy founded the town of Savoy, Texas, in 1872 when he sold forty acres of land to the Transcontinental Railroad (later the Texas and Pacific) on the condition that the new town bear his name. The college history (Boyd, 1939) states he was born in Mississippi in 1818 and came to Texas as a young man, while the memoir 'Before I Remember' — narrated by his daughter — gives his birth year as 1817 and states he died in 1889 at Joshua, Johnson County, Texas, of cancer; Boyd's text says he died August 1880 at Johnson City: these accounts conflict on both the death date and location. He served in the Confederate Army but did not see active service due to a foot wound, and crossed the Atlantic twice and the Pacific three times in later years.
- Sold forty acres to the Transcontinental Railroad (Texas and Pacific) in October 1872, founding the town of Savoy and having it named in his honor.
- Filed the city charter on April 15, 1872; sold town lots and donated the lot on which the Methodist Episcopal Church was built.
- Donated the land for the 1878 Methodist Church on Fowler Street, which became one of Savoy's oldest surviving structures.
- The 'Before I Remember' memoir (daughter's account) states he was born Aug. 2, 1817, died Sept. 1, 1887 at Goshee, Johnson County; Boyd's college history says born 1818, died August 1880 at Johnson City — a discrepancy in both birth year and death date/place.
- Marriage to Julia E. Davis solemnized March 5, 1874 in Fannin County by minister J. H. Ray.

### Julia E. Davis Savoy
Era: 1850-1899 · Roles: spouse of town founder · Also known as: Mrs. Julia E. Davis, Mrs. William Savoy
Julia E. Davis married Colonel William Savoy on March 5, 1874 in Fannin County, Texas. She had three children from a prior marriage to a Dr. Davis (Ernest, Irene, and Emma Marshall). She and Colonel Savoy had four children together including a daughter born November 26, 1874 in Savoy. After her husband's death she survived him by two decades according to Boyd; the memoir narrated by her daughter describes her as a woman of prominence in the Savoy community who recalled the town's welcome reception after their honeymoon.
- Married Col. William Savoy on March 5, 1874 in Fannin County, Texas.
- Mrs. Savoy survived her husband by two decades according to Boyd; her grave is dated August 7, 1908.

### Ada Savoy Travis
Era: 1850-1899 · Roles: daughter of town founder, family informant · Also known as: Mrs. Ada Savoy Travis
Ada Savoy Travis was a daughter of Colonel William Savoy and his second wife Tallulah Clark. She was born in Mississippi and received her education in Canton, Mississippi. She served as the primary informant for Boyd's 1939 college history on the subject of her father's life, recounting his California gold rush trip, Civil War service, and extensive travels.
- Provided recollections of her father Colonel Savoy's life to author Mattie Lee Boyd, cited as a key source for the biographical chapter.

### Nancy Savoy
Era: 2000-present · Roles: philanthropist, Savoy family descendant
Nancy Savoy, wife of the late John Savoy of Arizona, was the surviving spouse of the last remaining grandson of Colonel William Savoy. She donated $25,000 to Savoy Independent School District around 2004-2005 to fund the removal and restoration of the 1878 Methodist Church bronze bell and to establish a $1,000 annual scholarship. She and her late husband had reconnected with the Savoy community a few years prior to the donation.
- Donated $25,000 to Savoy ISD to restore the 1878 Methodist Church bell and fund a $1,000 annual scholarship.

### Marshall D. Savoy
Era: 1900-1949 · Born: 1876 (family genealogy) or c. 1920 (WWII-era officer) · Died: 1935 (family genealogy) · Roles: military officer, physician, World War II hero · Also known as: First Lt. Marshall D. Savoy, Marshall Durey Savoy
First Lt. Marshall D. Savoy was a Phoenix, Arizona native of the Savoy family and a battalion assistant surgeon in the U.S. Army during World War II. He is identified in family genealogy records as born March 21, 1876 in Savoy, Fannin County, Texas, and died March 21, 1935 in Phoenix. The 1946 Arizona Republic profile describes a Lt. Marshall D. Savoy who joined the regular army in January 1940 and served in the Pacific theater, earning two Silver Stars, the Soldier's Medal, and a Purple Heart with cluster for repeatedly risking his life to aid wounded comrades during the Philippine campaign; this Lt. Savoy may be his son or a closely related family member.
- Awarded two Silver Stars and the Soldier's Medal for heroism under fire as an assistant battalion surgeon during the Philippine campaign, 1945.
- Family genealogy form records Marshall Durey Savoy born March 21, 1876 in Savoy, Fannin County, Texas, died March 21, 1935, buried at Greenwood Cemetery, Phoenix, Arizona.

### Robert Roberson Halsell
Era: 1850-1899 · Born: September 28, 1848 · Died: 1924 · Roles: college president, college founder, educator · Also known as: R. R. Halsell, R. B. Halsell, Prof. Halsell, Professor Halsell, President Halsell
Robert Roberson Halsell was the founder and president of Savoy Male and Female College, organizing the school in 1876 and guiding it until its closure in the early 1890s. The college received its charter on January 22, 1880, naming Halsell and four co-trustees. The 1932 Sherman newspaper gives his first initial as 'R. H.' while Boyd's 1939 thesis uses 'R. B.' — a minor ambiguity in the sources. Born in Monroe County, Kentucky on September 28, 1848, he taught at Savoy and later in Anson, Texas, then moved to Durant, Oklahoma, where he was elected to the Oklahoma legislature; he died in Durant in 1924.
- Founded Savoy College in 1876 as a local school; expanded it to a chartered institution receiving its formal charter on January 22, 1880.
- Led the college through its most celebrated period; known for inspiring students with Latin phrases and 'fireside chat' speeches on self-improvement.
- Elected to the Oklahoma legislature after leaving Savoy; received a congratulatory letter signed by former students and prominent Grayson County citizens.
- Taught in Anson, Texas 1890-1892 after leaving Savoy, then returned briefly before continuing educational work in Oklahoma.
- Died in Durant, Oklahoma in 1924; survived by daughters Mrs. Annie Woodward of Durant and Mrs. Mollie Durant of Calera, Oklahoma, and son Doak Halsell.

### Lewis Holland
Era: 1850-1899 · Born: November 8, 1840 · Died: 1914 · Roles: college vice-president, professor, Baptist minister, newspaper founder · Also known as: Reverend Lewis Holland, Professor Holland, Rev. Lewis Holland
Lewis Holland was vice-president and co-principal of Savoy College, serving from the fall of 1886 to the spring of 1889. Born November 8, 1840, in Maury County, Tennessee, he served in Company D, Fourteenth Arkansas Confederate Infantry during the Civil War. He taught Latin, French, and higher mathematics at Savoy for ten or twelve years. He was one of the founders of the Baptist News (later the Baptist Standard), a major Texas Baptist newspaper, and served as vice-president of the State Baptist Convention, vice-president of Howard College, and president of the Board of Florence University. He died in San Antonio in 1914 at age 74.
- Co-founded the Baptist News at Honey Grove in December 1886 with J. H. Haines; the paper evolved into the Baptist Standard.
- Taught Latin, French, and higher mathematics at Savoy College for 10-12 years; served as vice-president under President Halsell.
- Served as faculty at Savoy from fall 1886 to spring 1889; replaced by Haskell.
- After Savoy, moved to Whitewright, then San Antonio, then Oklahoma; inherited wealth and died in 1914 age 74.

### James L. German
Era: 1850-1899 · Born: May 8, 1833 · Roles: college trustee, founder, farmer, educator · Also known as: J. L. German
James L. German was one of three founding trustees of Savoy Male and Female College, named in its January 22, 1880 charter. Born May 8, 1833, in Mercer County, Missouri, of Welsh descent, he taught school from the age of seventeen and earned his higher education at the University of Missouri. He came to Texas in 1874, married former pupil Eliza Paxton (daughter of Samuel S. Paxton), and became a prosperous farmer near Savoy. He helped finance many young people's educations, was a lifelong total abstainer, and was a supporter of Senator Joseph W. Bailey. He and his wife Eliza German had no children.
- Named as one of five original charter trustees of Savoy Male and Female College, charter granted January 22, 1880.
- Taught school starting at age seventeen; funded his higher education at the University of Missouri through teaching.
- Moved to Texas in 1874; farmed near Savoy and helped finance the education of many aspiring youth.

### Thomas J. Chenoweth
Era: 1850-1899 · Roles: college trustee, founder, farmer · Also known as: T. J. Chenoweth
Thomas J. Chenoweth was one of three founding trustees of Savoy Male and Female College, named in its January 22, 1880 charter. He was the son of Dr. John W. Chenoweth and grew up in southwestern Missouri before coming to Texas. In 1874 or 1876 he married a sister of James Paxton and a sister of Mrs. German, creating a close-knit family network among the college's founders. He was a devoted member of the Christian Church and a strong supporter of Christian education. His daughter Frankie was a piano teacher at the college in the late 1880s, and his daughter Tommie also taught in the Savoy schools.
- Named as one of five original charter trustees of Savoy Male and Female College, charter granted January 22, 1880.
- Son Frankie (daughter) taught piano at the College in the late 1880s; daughter Tommie also taught in Savoy schools.

### James Paxton
Era: 1850-1899 · Born: November 18, 1833 · Died: April 10, 1914 · Roles: college trustee, founder, farmer, stock breeder · Also known as: Mr. Paxton
James Paxton was one of three founding trustees of Savoy Male and Female College. Born November 18, 1833, the fifteenth child of William Casey and Mary Paxton of Kentucky, he came from a distinguished military family — his grandfather organized a company for General Jackson at New Orleans in 1814, and his father commanded volunteers in the Mexican War and served in the Kentucky legislature. He moved to Savoy around 1860-1870, became a fine stock breeder, and acquired about two thousand acres. He was a lifelong member of the Christian Church. He died April 10, 1914, in Tulsa (or Haskell), Texas, nearly 81 years old.
- Named as one of five original charter trustees of Savoy Male and Female College, charter granted January 22, 1880.
- Settled in Savoy around 1860-1870; became a prominent stock breeder and farmer.
- Died April 10, 1914 at nearly 81 years of age.

### Richard A. Gay
Era: 1850-1899 · Died: c. March 1887 · Roles: professor, minister · Also known as: Dick Gay, R. A. Gay, Rev. R. A. Gay
Richard A. Gay, universally known as 'Dick' Gay, was one of the earliest faculty members at Savoy College. He came to Texas from Georgia at age four and attended public schools in Fannin County before studying at Savoy College. He conducted the Primary Department of the College as early as 1881-1882 and taught several years. He was ordained as a minister in 1880 at Ladonia, Fannin County. For the 1885-1886 school year he served on the faculty of the State Normal College at Athens, Texas, teaching mathematics. He died of heart failure, reportedly at the end of March 1887.
- Conducted the Primary Department of Savoy College as early as 1881-1882; taught several years while also advancing his own studies.
- Served on faculty at the State Normal College at Athens, Texas in 1885-1886 as teacher of mathematics.

### Edward H. Pritchett
Era: 1850-1899 · Born: December 24, 1824 · Died: June 9, 1925 · Roles: professor, civil engineer, lawyer · Also known as: E. H. Pritchett, Professor Pritchett
Edward H. Pritchett was the oldest of five children born December 24, 1824 in Fayette County, Georgia. He attended public schools in Fannin County, continued at Savoy College, then graduated from Alabama Polytechnic Institute in January 1879 with a B.E. degree. After the destruction of Savoy College, he took up the study of law and was admitted to the Texas bar. He served many years as steward and Sunday School superintendent at Bonham and was a member of the Methodist Episcopal Church. He died June 9, 1925.
- Graduated from Alabama Polytechnic Institute in 1880 with a B.E. degree; taught at Savoy College before pursuing law and civil engineering.
- Died June 9, 1925 at Bonham, Texas after long career as steward and Sunday School superintendent at Bonham Methodist Episcopal Church.

### Edward P. Montgomery
Era: 1850-1899 · Born: c. 1848 · Roles: professor, physician · Also known as: Pink Montgomery, E. P. Montgomery, Dr. Edward P. Montgomery
Edward P. ('Pink') Montgomery was a brief but well-remembered faculty member at Savoy College. Born in Pikesville, Tennessee, he came to Texas in 1868 and attended Savoy Male and Female College under President Halsell. In 1883 he graduated with honors from Vanderbilt University Medical School. He commenced practice as a physician and became a rising figure among Texas surgeons. His long service as a physician in Grayson County ended in a train wreck at Whitesboro; his widow died in 1925.
- Graduated with honors from Vanderbilt University in 1883; practiced medicine in Grayson County, Texas.
- Attended Savoy Male and Female College under President Halsell; briefly served on faculty as a teacher.
- His son Crawford Blair Montgomery was connected to the college history; he died in a train wreck at Whitesboro.

### John Thomas Ashcraft
Era: 1850-1899 · Died: February 9, 1920 · Roles: professor, lawyer, public health advocate · Also known as: J. T. Ashcraft, Mr. Ashcraft
John Thomas Ashcraft was a faculty member at Savoy College and Polytechnic College who became one of the more prominent alumni in public life. Born in Clay County, Alabama, he came to Texas early in life, graduated from Polytechnic, and studied medicine in Louisville, earning his medical degree in 1887. He married Miss Aggie McKee on December 16, 1885. He taught mathematics and natural science at both Polytechnic College and Savoy Male and Female College. He was a member of the State Anti-Tuberculosis Commission and undertook major public health campaigns until his death on February 9, 1920. Ten children were born to him and Mrs. Ashcraft.
- Served on the State Anti-Tuberculosis Commission and led public health campaigns until his death.
- Taught higher mathematics, natural science, and education at Polytechnic College and Savoy Male and Female College.
- Died February 9, 1920; son Cyrus Andrew was at one time a vice-president of United States Steel Corporation.

### Leonidas William Pierce
Era: 1850-1899 · Born: October 15, 1870 · Died: July 16, 1922 · Roles: professor, missionary, student · Also known as: L. W. Pierce, Lee Pierce, Professor W. Pierce
Leonidas William Pierce was born October 15, 1870, to Sidney Pierce and Cornelia Brown. He attended Savoy College as a student, earned his A.B. degree in 1883, and was immediately appointed to the faculty to teach English, German, and later natural science. He served as senior assistant editor and business manager of the student paper, the Philomean Messenger. He completed his theological training at the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary in Louisville, Kentucky in 1892 and was sent as a missionary to Yangchow, China. He married Miss Molly E. Tupper in 1893 and spent thirty-one years as a missionary in China, establishing two churches in Yangchow and twelve others in nearby cities. He drowned July 16, 1922, at Soochow, China when a launch capsized, and is buried at Chinkiang.
- Received A.B. degree from Savoy College in 1883; appointed to faculty as teacher of English, German, and natural science.
- Served as missionary in Yangchow, China for thirty-one years, establishing two churches in Yangchow and twelve others in surrounding cities and towns.
- Drowned July 16, 1922 at Soochow, China; buried at Chinkiang, China.

### Will I. Hunt
Era: 1850-1899 · Born: May 9, 1861 · Roles: professor, student, county official · Also known as: W. I. Hunt, Will Hunt
Will I. Hunt was born May 9, 1861, near the present town of Whitewright, Texas. He entered Savoy College in 1882, excelled in the Victorian Literary Society, and served as editor of the Philomean Messenger in his final year. He graduated with an A.B. degree in spring 1887 — the second alumnus of Savoy College to complete the A.B. course — and was invited to join the teaching staff, teaching Greek and higher mathematics. He later moved to Stephenville, Texas where he served over forty years in various county offices including County Surveyor, County Clerk, District Clerk, and County Commissioner. He was, per the 1939 thesis, the only surviving member of the regular College faculty at the time of writing.
- Second alumnus of Savoy College to complete the A.B. degree (1887); immediately joined faculty as instructor in Greek and higher mathematics.
- Served as County Surveyor, County Clerk, District Clerk, and County Commissioner in Stephenville, Texas over more than forty years.
- Married Miss Mollie Lee on August 9, 1888; celebrated their golden wedding anniversary on August 9, 1938.

### Elbu Bennett Hinshaw
Era: 1850-1899 · Born: October 14, 1860 · Died: June 27, 1920 · Roles: professor, educator, seminary superintendent · Also known as: E. B. Hinshaw, Elbu B. Hinshaw, Professor Hinshaw
Elbu Bennett Hinshaw was born October 14, 1860, in Indiana, of Pennsylvania Quaker ancestry. He graduated from Hiwasee College in Tennessee in 1884 with a B.S. degree and received an M.A. from the same institution. He joined the Savoy College faculty in early 1887 to fill the vacancy in classical languages left by Professor Holland's retirement. In 1887 he was selected to become president of Bloomfield Seminary in Panola County, Chickasaw Nation, an educational institution for young Indian women. He remained superintendent for eighteen years, married twice, and later taught at Southeastern State Normal School at Durant. He was a close friend of Governor Johnston, the last chief of the Chickasaw Nation. He died in Oklahoma City, June 27, 1920, and was buried in Collinsville, Texas.
- Joined Savoy College faculty in 1887 to teach classical languages; served from 1887 to near the college's closure.
- Became superintendent of Bloomfield Seminary in the Chickasaw Nation in 1887, serving eighteen years and building it to 220 students.
- Died June 27, 1920 in Oklahoma City; buried in Collinsville, Texas.

### Alexander C. Bohannon
Era: 1850-1899 · Born: July 22, 1820 · Died: c. 1895-1896 · Roles: professor, educator · Also known as: A. C. Bohannon, Professor Bohannon
Alexander C. Bohannon was a Virginian born July 22, 1820, in Mathews County, Virginia, the son of Reuben W. and Nancy Bohannon. He was a graduate of Hampden-Sidney College and studied law before the Civil War, during which he served in the Confederate cavalry. He taught at schools in North Carolina, Indiana, Kentucky, and various Texas institutions before being invited by Professor Holland to fill the chair of logic and rhetoric at Savoy College around 1881. He remained at Savoy until the college closed in 1890, then served as superintendent of schools at Bells, Texas for two years. He died around 1895 or 1896. His daughter Hattie Donovan Bohannon authored the novel 'A Second Marriage' (G. W. Dillingham, New York, 1909).
- Filled chair of logic and rhetoric at Savoy College from approximately 1881 through the college's closure in 1890.

### 1930-news-cyclonepoem (1930) — CYCLONE ANNIVERSARY (poem)
CYCLONE ANNIVERSARY

Time moves along with rapid pace
    On its incessant way,
Sixty full years have made their score
    Since that sad day in May.

It was the twenty-eighth of May—
    After the sunset's glow
Had faded from the golden west,
    The storm began to blow.

Few realized the danger great,
    Which in those dark clouds lay,
As no roaring, freakish cyclone
    Had ever passed this way.

'Twas only seconds till the town
    A pile of debris made,
Lives were snuffed out, some instantly,
    And houses low were laid.

From neighbor towns and cities
    Came food and clothing, too,
Also many willing workers
    Who knew just what to do.

Soon dead were buried, wounded ones
    Were rescued from its wake
And surrounded with such comforts
    As loving friends could make.

Eye witnesses are growing few,
    Soon they will all be gone,
But never will the memory die,
    It passes on and on.

                        M. May

### 1932-news-halsell (1932) — Old Savoy College Founded in 1876
HERMAN DAILY DEMOCRAT, SHERMAN, TEXAS

Old Savoy College Founded in 1876

Special to The Democrat

SAVOY, Texas. Savoy college was organized in 1876 by Prof. Robert H. Halsell. For four years the institution was conducted by its founder and owner as a local school for the children of families in the small towns and settlements of the region.

Then in 1880, when Prof. Halsell's reputation as a teacher of mathematics and English literature had spread all over North Texas, he decided to enlarge the scope of the bookkeeping and courses more suitable for boys' education were offered.

The institution was granted Jan. 22, 1880, to R. H. Halsell, Lewis Holland, T. J. Chenoweth, James Paxson, and J. L. German. The institution was called Savoy Male and Female college. The purpose for which the charter was founded was "to maintain and support an institution of learning for the education of males and females and to empower the faculty to confer the usual college degrees."

The college prospered and gained renown in North Texas and eventually throughout the South. It had the most exceptional library of any school in Texas at that time, with the exception of the State University at Austin.

LEWIS HOLLAND          ROBERT H. HALSELL

BELL OF OLD COLLEGE IN THIS SCHOOL BUILDING

"Though its glory lives on," one former student has written, "its active days came to a tragic end in the vacation days of 1896, the college building burned and only a heap of ashes and debris was left to greet the eyes of those who loved it so dearly."

After the fire, the college was not rebuilt. Only its influence, as expressed in the lives of its pupils, remains to speak of its service to Texas and the southwest.

Lewis Holland was co-laborer with Prof. Halsell when the college charter was granted. He taught Latin, French and higher mathematics for 10 or 12 years and resigned to enter other activities. He retired from Savoy about 1901 and has lived his life all his life.

After severing his connection with the college, Mr. Holland moved to Whitewright and later to San Antonio and Oklahoma. He inherited some wealth and died in 1914 at the age of 74. One son, Cecil Moore, of Holland, lives in San Antonio and a daughter, Mrs. McKinnon, is a resident of Sherman.

After the college burned, Prof. Halsell taught for a while in the Savoy school district. Then he went to Durant, Okla., where he taught there a few years. He died there in 1924 along with some of his family. Surviving him are two daughters, Mrs. Annie Woodward of Durant, and Mrs. Mollie Durant of Calera, Okla., and one son, Doak Halsell of Durant.

A handsome building was erected on the old site in the southeast part of town but a frame structure was built on the north side of Main Street of the town, which is now highway 69, when the present modern, commodious building (shown above) was erected on the same site.

That frame structure was replaced by a two-story brick, which in a few years was inadequate for the growing needs of the school community and the present modern, commodious building (shown above) was erected on the same site.

It is now being supplemented by a native rock athletic building that has been named Halsell gymnasium. Plans are being made to hold the reunion of ex-students of the old college when the memorial building is dedicated, which will likely be in the early spring.

What brought a world traveler, distinguished frontiersman, perhaps one of the finest horsemen or counterspys in American history, has never been recorded. And those who might have had the answer are long since dead.

Col. Savoy was born Aug. 2, 1817 in Mississippi and he died Sept. 1, 1887 in Johnson County, Tex.

He came to Fannin County in 1870 after he had "been everywhere and done everything." He bought the land that ultimately became the site for James William Smith, who according to legend, was about to lose it. He paid for it with gold nuggets he had dug out of the earth in California in 1849.

RAILROAD CONTRACT

In 1872, Col. Savoy made a contract with the Transcontinental Railroad, the forerunner of the Texas & Pacific, to give them 40 acres of land in the center of his holdings, provided they would take the initative in building a town. The deed was formally entered Oct. 29, 1873 in Bonham.

On April 15, 1873 the charter for the town of Savoy was granted and lots sold for from $150 to $500, proving to be another gold mine for the intrepid Col. Savoy.

Where Col. Savoy got his rank isn't known. He enlisted in the Confederate Army in the Civil War, but received a foot injury and never saw active service.

Col. Savoy made the trek to California by water when word of the gold rush swept the nation and he was one of the more fortunate miners. He brought his gold back in heavy cotton bags, twice being robbed en route to his native Mississippi, the first time by Indians and the second time by Mexi-cans. But they missed most of the colonel's "cotton-s a c k gold."

INTERNATIONAL SPY?

After the colonel's civil war injury, he started on his international travels that saw him twice cross the Atlantic and three times the Pacific, doing, according to a daughter of the colonel's much later, secret work for the United States.

On one of his trips a storm hit the ship and damaged the sails to the extent that it drifted three months before it was repaired and the voyage continued. On another of his voyages, all of the crew and passengers were stricken with cholera while in port and he was the lone survivor.

MEET AGAIN

Four years after he came to Fannin County, Col. Savoy, then a widower, married Mrs. Julia Duncan, a widow with three children. Out of five children, only two of the colonel's were alive at the time.

C. C. Waldrum, who lived just east of the toppled tower, got the brunt of the water.

Town Reconstructed

Reconstruction began at once. The first two blocks, running north and south on Water street, and as many directions now. As the town progressed, and the west block of bricks on the north side of the street were torn away, the buildings were built. One by one the old wood buildings were torn away, and the bricks took their places. Two or three of the old buildings to be torn down are the Drugstore building owned by the late W. L. Teague. After Mr. Teague's death P. O. Teague took over the cleaning. It stood north of the H. H. Arterberry store.

The Gooseberry has served Savoy and the surrounding community in the drug and grocery trade for almost half a century. He established the business in 1892, in what was almost an unpopulated place. After the establishment of the Savoy Tribune, later known as the Savoy Star, H. H. Arterberry was connected with it for a time. After his withdrawal, the newspaper was operated until his son Max was through school, and was made a partner. It now operates under the name Arterberry and Son. Mr. Arterberry was born and raised in Savoy. He has education in the Savoy College.

The Savoy Tribune, later known as the Savoy Star, was first published about 1890, and by 1902, when T. E. Arterberry and L. H. King established the Savoy Star. Mr. King operated the Savoy Star only a short time, then Mr. Arterberry bought out King. Arterberry continued the work together for 23 years when he was claimed by his calling. He left the family alone until her marriage to J. B. May, who is her assistant. The family is dry now and has a Washington hand press of which there is one of the best in the state here today. Besides the local news, advertising, and editorials, Mrs. May has tried to save local history, aided by many who are compiling scrap-books.

College Established

The Savoy College was established in 1880 when Prof. J. L. Halsell, served as a local school for Savoy and surrounding communities. Through the years Prof. Halsell decided to enlarge the school into a college and applied to the state for a charter, which was granted Jan. 22, 1880, to H. R. Halsell and others; also C. E. Paxton and J. L. German, with the name "Savoy Female College" or "Female College." It is spoken of today as the Savoy College or the Halsell College. The school, much like the school of today, consisted of a grammar school, high school and college all in one system.

The highest degree to be conferred was an A. B. degree. The school did not put much stress on athletics. There were literary and debating societies were more popular. The school had two literary societies, the Platahian, for the older boys, and the Philomathians for the younger ones. The students were made "Keepers of Governmental Laws." Under this system each student was required to write an essay each week. A lady of Spanish descent taught French and Spanish. Among those taught in the school during its 14 years were H. R. ### elk-26 — Savoy Star's 110-Year-Old Press Is Now Museum Piece
Savoy Star's 110-Year-Old Press Is Now Museum Piece

The century-old Washington hand press on which the Savoy Star was printed for 41 years until publication was suspended with the June 25 issue, has been purchased from Mrs. J. B. May by the Sherman Democrat. It will be held as a museum piece because it is the same type of press on which the Democrat was printed in its early years, beginning August 14, 1879, and because of the possibility this was the original Democrat press itself.

One of the oldest pieces of printing equipment in the country, the Washington hand press was invented in 1827 by Samuel Rust of New York, but he sold his invention to R. Hoe and Company, who began to manufacture with some improvements. The Savoy Star press bears the legend "IM" in raised letters, a part of the foundry pattern to show it was one of the first thousand presses of its kind made. In depressed figures, the number of the press is 123, showing that it was the 123rd press of its kind made.

An investigation by the Hoe company in the spring of 1939 revealed that the press was made just before 1830 or shortly thereafter and that by 1850 relatively few presses of the type were manufactured.

Another evidence of its early manufacture is the fact that it does not carry the name of the Hoe company, the only legend outside of numbering being the descriptive name Washington Press. It does carry a design of an eagle with extended wings and, on later presses, instead of the name of the press instead of the name of the company, a copy of the American shield. Later presses carried the name of the manufacturer instead of the name of the press, and the faces of the last presses of the late presses were raised figures of the head of George Washington.

A quarter of a century ago the Washington press was beginning to disappear in print shops. A few remain in use now as proof presses. One side of a form can be done at a time, and the service of two persons is required, a operator one to ink the type with a roller and another to pull the lever drawing the weight of the press against the type to form a print.

G. O. Hunter, the North Binkley, one of the founders of the Sherman Democrat who retired in 1932, recalls that a Washington hand press was the principal piece of equipment for The Democrat for some years.

The Democrat's hand press was purchased from Captain Crooke, who was operating the Sherman Courier, a morning newspaper here, later taken to Whitewright by Mr. Hunter and his brother for the Whitewright Democrat, the forerunner of the Sherman Democrat. Some years after the Sherman Daily Democrat was established in 1881, this press was taken to Savoy by Hunter's brother-in-law, Elder Hunter, elder brother of the two founders of the Sherman Democrat, then to Whitewright and later to Caddo, Okla.

The Savoy Star's press was purchased from the late Zeb Spearman, who operated the weekly paper at Whitewright. Attempt has been made to identify the Savoy Democrat as the Savoy Star press but there is no established fact.

It is considered possible, however, that the press was brought back from Caddo, Okla. to Whitewright and used on the Plow and Hammer or the Whitewright Sun by Spearman.

The Hoe company wrote to The Democrat: "We continue to receive many letters from users of these old presses but to date we can not recall any that has had a machine number that is as low as that on the Star press."

### elk-39 (Sept. 26, 1952) — Former Traveling Salesman Recalls Good Old Country Peddling Times
Page 6—THE DENISON HERALD—Friday, Sept. 26, 1952

SAVOY'S FORMER TRAVELING SALESMAN, L. A. Brown, eyes a piece of expert needle work done by his wife, who took up that work after suffering a stroke in 1944.

MARRIED FARMER'S DAUGHTER

Former Traveling Salesman Recalls Good Old Country Peddling Times

BY JOHN CLIFT
(Herald State Editor)

SAVOY—One of the last of the old time country peddlers looked back on the "good old days" this week and recollected that the country might be better off if the old peddlers were back in the harness.

L. A. Brown put in some seven years at the turn of the century driving a general merchandise wagon, one of nine such men working for B. C. H. Bradford of Whitewright.

"I put the butter in a large can I carried," he said.

"And it was a long way from being fresh country butter by the time he got back," put in Mrs. Brown.

Forty-five years ago this Feb. 2, will mark the anniversary of the traveling salesman's marriage to the farmer's daughter, in this case the former Miss Weston Jenkins.

Less than a year later he quit the road to become a farmer "something that I've liked better than anything I ever did," he recalled.

SUCCESSFUL FARMER

Brown was a success as a farmer, having his best luck tilling the soil during a 10-year stretch spent in West Texas "where the cotton made two bales to an acre."

However, his love for North Texas plus the fact that his children had grown up and married and lived in the area, brought him back to Savoy and he finished up his career on the farm south of Savoy.

Mrs. Brown suffered a stroke in 1944 and has been confined to a wheel chair ever since. As a result, a daughter-in-law, Mrs. Paul Brown who lives down the street a block or so, comes over at least three times each day. She prepares all of the meals, does all of the housework for the old couple, and, if the occasion warrants, spends the night.

Mrs. Brown has been keeping herself amused since she suffered her stroke by painting and doing needlework.

OWN DESIGNER

She creates her own patterns for her needlework and then puts the patterns on hand towels.

She uses some water color, but mostly crayon or pencil in her drawings. She uses only her imagination and has come up with several lively crayon landscapes.

Too, the portrait of her husband, done from memory, is excellent. When questioned about the amount of hair she put on her husband's head, she replied "that's why I married him in the first place. He had the most beautiful head of hair I ever saw on a man."

Mrs. Brown did one bit of cheating on her art when she peeked into a mirror to complete her self portrait.

"I just couldn't get my chin and mouth right," she defended herself.

Her unusual designs on hand towels has created a waiting list among North Texans who have seen and admired her work. They are especially in demand around the holiday seasons when her handiwork finds its way under many Christmas trees.

WON A SUIT

All of her work has been done since she suffered the stroke, which left her with her left arm paralyzed.

The Browns are looking forward to celebrating their golden wedding anniversary five years hence.

"I might make enough to get my husband a new suit," grinned Mrs. Brown.

"She's kidding me about the suit I won for being the best traveling salesman back before we were married," smiled her husband. "I got a $37.50 suit that was a dandy."

While the Browns rely on their daughter-in-law to keep their house cleaned and to prepare their meals, both still are able to get around very well. Mrs. Brown is 82, her husband 78.

"And I'm feeling so good if I'm not careful I may wind up looking for a job," he concluded.

### elk-41 (Aug. 15) — 'Uncle Hade' Whitsett's Death Thins Ranks Of Civil War Vets
'Uncle Hade' Whitsett's Death Thins Ranks Of Civil War Vets

BONHAM, Aug. 15. (P)—Joseph Haden Whitsett, 103, one of Texas' three surviving Confederate veterans, died today at his home here.

Almost blind and deaf, he had made his home with his niece, Mrs. Zora Arledge.

Even in the past few years he had liked to have visitors and talk about the old days.

He served with Shelby's Escort in the Missouri Cavalry during the Civil War.

His 104th birthday was little more than a month away — Sept. 18.

Whitsett's death leaves only Walter W. (Colonel Will) Williams of Franklin and Thomas Evans Riddle of Austin of Texas' once numerous legions of Confederate veterans.

Whitsett's declining years were sad compared with the happy days of only a few years ago, when he liked nothing better than a noisy, knee-slapping round of checkers.

In recent years he was confined to his bed.

Whitsett was born at Glasgow, Ky., on Sept. 18, 1847. He was the son of Dr. William and Elizabeth Edmund Whitsett. He came with his parents to Texas in 1853. They settled in the English community southeast of Bonham.

It was on May 6, 1864, that he enlisted in the Confederate Army. He returned home a year and a month later. Then he attended Carlton College here. He got permission to carry a gun to classes.

Before the Texas and Pacific Railway came into Bonham, Whitsett hauled freight by wagon. After marrying Miss Betty Stone, March 18, 1873, he went into the mercantile business at Dodd City. About 1900 he returned to Bonham.

He had made his home with Mrs. Arledge since his wife died in 1931.

Whitsett never fired a shot in the Civil War.

"The only thing that bothered me during the war," he told reporters in 1948, "was the itch. The itch and creepers. Did you ever see a body louse? Well, in those days we called them creepers."

He broke his hip shortly before his 98th birthday — an accident usually fatal to a man of that advanced age. But Uncle Hade recovered from that.

He was a 50-year Mason, an Odd Fellow, and a lifetime member of the Bonham Volunteer Fire Department.

He had no children. Several nieces live in the Bonham area.

### elk-50 — A. Deatherage To Observe 94th Birthday On March 22 / Savoy Couple Soon Will Observe 67th Wedding Anniversary
SAVOY PIONEERS—Mr. and Mrs. A. Deatherage of Savoy are among the oldest married couples in North Texas. They will observe their 67th wedding anniversary next August. Mr. Deatherage will be 94 years old March 22. Mrs. Deatherage will observe her 86th birthday Aug. 8.

A. Deatherage To Observe 94th Birthday On March 22

Savoy Couple Soon Will Observe 67th Wedding Anniversary

By MISS ELIZABETH LARGE
Herald Correspondent

SAVOY, March 7—A. Deatherage, who will observe his ninety-fourth birthday March 22, is a Savoy pioneer in the truest sense of the word. He has lived in this community 89 years and has seen progress made over that span of years.

A retired farmer and painter, Mr. Deatherage was born near Sweetwater, Tenn., March 22, 1848. He came to Texas with his parents, Mr. and Mrs. John E. Deatherage, when he was 5 years old. The family settled five miles west of here and he has not lived further away from Savoy during the 89 years.

Freighted Supplies

As a young man he freighted supplies from Jefferson and other distant points with an ox team. The round trips often required weeks and often disaster marred the trip before reaching home. He recalls an accident once when crossing a river which almost cost him his life. One ox drowned and a wagon load of supplies was lost.

His hobby is music. He was leader of the "Sacred Harp Leaders" and it is believed he is the only member of that group now living. He still walks to town almost every day in pretty weather.

Have Seen Savoy Grow

He was married to Miss Sally Ray, daughter of Mr. and Mrs. J. W. Ray, Aug. 19, 1875, at Savoy. She was born Aug. 8, 1856, and is Savoy's oldest woman. Her hobby is flowers and quilting. Her sweetpeas are the most beautiful in the community and each year are shared by her friends. They have spent their entire married life in Savoy, the last 50 years at their present home.

They have seen Savoy grow from open country to a little town. A modern concrete highway today marks the trails when he came here. They have used candles for lighting in their early married life but their home is equipped with electric lights today. In earlier days they waited weeks for mail, but it is delivered to their front door daily now.

They began life at the peak of the horse and buggy days, but today modern automobiles whiz by their home all hours of the day and night.

Eight of their 10 children are living. They are Mrs. Rose Mathis of Texarkane, Mrs. Laura Carter of Dallas, Frank Deatherage and Fred Deatherage of Hugo, Okl., Mrs. Alfa Jenkins of Savoy, Mrs. Montia Houston, of Slaton, Mrs. Bernidene Hastings of Longview and Mrs. Annie Tyer of Hugo. They have 15 grandchildren and 10 great-grandchildren. Mr. Deatherage has a brother, Dr. William Deatherage, at Dallas.

Both are members of the Primitive Baptist Church, both their fathers having served as ministers of that church.

Mrs. Aditha Woods

### elk-67 — Tiny Savoy Doing He Man's Job In Giving Men For Uncle Sam
Tiny Savoy Doing He Man's Job In Giving Men For Uncle Sam

BY JOHN CLIFT
(Herald State Editor)

SAVOY— One of the smallest towns in North Texas is supplying one of the largest amounts of manpower for the armed forces in the nation. On a percentage basis, that is.

The latest head count in Savoy listed the population at 312. As of next week when Lewis Beckham reports to the air force at Lackland in San Antonio, some 33 Savoy residents will be currently in service.

"Somebody ought to give us an 'E' for excellence in supplying men for the armed forces," said Postmaster Claud Strickland, who, as postmaster, keeps probably as close a watch as anyone on men's immediate families.

Of Savoy men in service, two have been killed, both in the early days in service in Africa. They were Peter Farr, Pan Seals, and Wirt Terry.

However, another died overseas in World War I. He was Isaac Le Large, who succumbed to pneumonia.

SON-IN-LAWS, TOO

Several families have two or more sons in service and two families with one have additional male members 1-A.

Mrs. Fae Miller has two sons and a son-in-law in uniform. Sgt. Bill is in the air force at Laredo and Capt. Sam is now on his third overseas hitch, this time in Japan. The son-in-law is Joe Bailey Springfield, who also is in Japan.

Another brother combine is Harry and Jimmy Arterbery, both serving in Japan. They are the sons of Bob Elk.

SAVOY CYCLONE — 1879 / 1880

"After a residence of five years in Denison, I moved to Savoy, a station on the Transcontinental line, 11 miles from Bonham, and I was in the mercantile business there when the cyclone struck in 1879 or 1800. I retired early on the night of the cyclone which came at about 10 o'clock. Before I dosed off to sleep I cautioned my wife, who was in the room with a glass oil lamp in her hand. I told her she was liable to set the house on fire. The next thing I knew I went out of bed as if by a force not my own and fell sprawling on the floor. What appeared to be a terrific hailstorm was raging. Hailstones weighing ten pounds each were bombarding the house. I opened the door and at once realized that tremendous mischief was afoot. By the incessant flashes of lightning which made a continuous light, I saw the railroad station vantish, perceived that the business district of the town was bare of houses and realized that what I took to be hailstones striking the house were the flying shreds and fragments of demolished buildings. The wind was so strong that I could not shut the door. I called Harry Naylor my nephew to assist me. The blow was all over in a minute or two. Then came a veritable deluge of rain, a regular waterspout. We could hear on all sides cries of men, women and children in distress.

"Going to the rescue Harry and I found the cyclone had made a path about 250 yards in width through the town, carrying away every business house and every dwelling in its path except mine. We began to pick up the injured and to carry them into my house. Before long people of the town living outside the path of the storm came to our assistance. We worked all night, but were unable to afford much relief. There were two physicians in the town and one of them was so old and feeble he was of little use. It was not until the next day that relief trains came from Sherman, Denison and Bonham bringing surgeons and supplies. Men, women and children with fractured limbs and other injuries went all that time without surgical attention.

"Eleven persons were killed by the cyclone and five others died later of injuries, making a total of 16. I do not remember how many were injured. The population was about 300. Of the dead and injured, not one had a stitch of clothing on and many who escaped injury found themselves naked in the street. The rescuers wrapped the injured in bed sheets or quilts as they picked them up. Strange as it may seem, I have no recollection of the funeral services that must have been held during the two or three days following the calamity. A situation such as we had that night is calculated to open new founts of emotion in a man. If you ever go through a cyclone, the fear of another one goes ghosting through your head every time the wind begins to blow hard."   BY W. S. ADAIR AND J. M. NAYLOR

EDUCATION

While there was no organized system of education in Texas when the first settlers came to Fannin County, all the families knew the value of education and worked to provide some sort of academic training for their children. The first schools were neighborhood schools, established by churches or people in the communities and parents usually paid a great deal of the credit must go to local men who located school, sometimes several families joined together to hire a teacher to educate their children. Many times the teacher boarded with the families and received their pay in farm produce.

In many villages school buildings were simple log cabins usually with split-log seats, slab tables and dirt floors. Even the larger and better schools had poor equipment and the length of school days and school terms varied depending on weather, the threat of Indian attack, or the planting and harvesting when the children were needed at home.

A typical school day was spent learning the bedrock three R's — reading, 'riting, 'rithmetic — with an occasional subject as history, geography, and such subjects as art. Great importance was placed on repetition and memory work. Students recited their lessons aloud in unison. The rod was a widely accepted educational practice by educational axiom practiced by every teacher of the day, and the importance placed by every child switch was very much a part of the normal day as were the grueling spelling bees and debates during school hours.

SAVOY MALE AND FEMALE COLLEGE

Savoy Male and Female College was probably the first collegiate coeducational school in Texas to admit men and women to the same classes on the same academic standards. Founded in 1876 at Savoy, the college was chartered in 1880 by Robert R. Halsell, president, Lewis Holland, vice president and James L. German, Thomas Chenoweth and James Paxton, trustees. By 1885 the college was conferring B. S. and A. B. degrees. The A. B. degree required four years each of Latin, Greek and mathematics with courses in physiology, physics, chemistry, rhetoric and elocution. By 1887, 1888 enrollment was 66 with 102 and 133 pupils in the primary and preparatory departments. During the same years 44 Indian students, one of course came from across the Red River with federal funds for tuition.

Academic and social standards were strict with social contact between men and women students rigidly forbidden except on carefully chaperoned occasions. Extracurricular interests produced a dramatic club, debates and three literary societies, of which one published monthly "The Platonian Messenger", a combination literary magazine-newspaper.

The college building burned in 1890 and the school was not rebuilt until 1892. The founder is commemorated in the Halsell Memorial Gymnasium built for the Savoy public school through the Works Progress Administration funds in 1937. The Savoy Alumni Association, organized in 1937 with 128 members, has held annual reunions.

LADONIA MALE AND FEMALE INSTITUTE

Founded in 1860 by the Sister Grove Baptist Association comprised of 23 churches in seven counties, the Ladonia Male and Female Institute was organized in Ladonia instead of Bonham at the last minute because it offered a two-story building and more than $2,600 in donations. It then conducted a private school in the building and became the first principal with J. C. Averett becoming principal in 1861.

The school was moved in 1863 to a similar facility but soon closed because of inadequate enrollment. It was reopened by W. B. Featherstone in 1867 and returned to Ladonia in 1868. The school was finally closed in 1875 because of insufficient funding.

Upholstering, Scouting, Hobbies Of Space Scientist's Family

By Audrey McAlister

Their dad's three weeks vacation is not a time for "all play" for the children of Mr. and Mrs. Jim Ralph Elk of Friendswood, near the NASA Space Center in Houston.

While visiting their grandparents, Mr. and Mrs. Jack Harden in Bonham, and their father's mother, Mrs. R. C. Elk in Savoy, they are helping their parents upholster their grandmother's overstuffed furniture. And they love every minute of it.

Elk, who is head of the Lu[nar Trajectories Studies at the Space Center], took up the hobby of upholstering as a family project, as he and his wife believe in children learning to work and he busy part of the time, and they also believe in their earning some of their spending money.

In "the hobby house" which they built on the back of their property in Friendswood, they have turned in many professional jobs and also used it to aid their neighbors in re-doing their furniture when their house flooded. A wedding present sofa which the family built from the oak frame to the complete cut velvet upholstery, was admired by a neighbor who offered them $1,000 for it—but it wasn't for sale.

Completing his eighth year with the Space Flight Center, Elk is a little at a disadvantage talking to a layman about his responsibilities as head of the Lunar Trajectories studies. Most of it is over their heads. He and his crew of 30 designed and mapped the mathematical procedures for certain very complicated trajectory developments for the lunar landings. They were completed and computer programmed months ahead of the launches. You can bet they were right there watching the launchings, orbits and landings in case they were needed and just wouldn't miss it for the world, as they are a part of "the team."

While growing up, he admits he never dreamed of an opportunity to be a part of the team at NASA. And he insists that it is really "a team effort." The past successes and those of the future depend upon the team of men collected from science, flight, engineering and ever[y] contractors, he says.

Elk also believes that some practical advantages will come to the United States. Some advantages have already shown the way to more explorations and benefits. He and the other key men involved in the program believe that, like all new ventures undertaken by man in exploration and discovery, time, effort, and enterprise will prove their worth in effort and expense in first hand knowledge of outer space for the future.

But when this physicist begins to talk about Scouting, he is almost as enthusiastic as he is about outer space!

He is a graduate of East Texas State University, where he earned both his B. S. and Masters in Physics. His wife is the former Anita Harden, daughter of the Jack Hardens and a 1953 graduate of Bonham high school. Before going with the [Space Center, he was involved in the development of cyrogenic aeronautical rocket engines for Lewis Research Institute in Cleveland, O.]

But, Scouting, now, that is another matter! As a Scout leader he speaks of taking the eldest son, James, canoeing down the Rio Grande river, he talks of camping trips, and all that Scouting involves. He points out with pride that James, 15, is an Eagle Scout, Lee, who is 12, is a First Class Scout, Leslie, 11, is in the Webelos, and Bruce, 8, is in the Cub Scouts. Denita, the only girl in the family, is six, and planning to be a Camp Fire Girl or Girl Scout.

James makes wooden antique purses and the whole family is also involved in making vinyl and other handbags. They have sold over 100 of the handbags already. Even the youngest child, Gary, almost 4, can pull pass tools and earns part of his allowance.

Living in the Houston, Galveston area, they also enjoy beach parties and are involved in the work of United Methodist church, and enjoy many other activities. This family practices and resists the "togetherness." They always visit in Bonham and Savoy during the Christmas and summer holidays.

### beforeiremember_p1 — Before I Remember
Chapter I – Before I Remember

"My Grandfather", she begins, "fled from France after Napoleon's disaster at Waterloo. He settled in the Mississippi Territory and, along with many other of the Little Emperor's Supporters, did all he could to further the plot to bring Bonaparte to New Orleans from St. Helena. After Napoleon's death, he decided to stay in the New World. In 1817 he claimed a large tract of land near Natchez on the Mississippi River; married that same year and in the following year, my Father was born; christened impressively William Louis Marshall Savoy, so named for one of Napoleon's fine Marshals. When he was eight years old, his mother was killed when a wagon in which she and grandfather were riding overturned. Grandfather married a year later (1826) and after two unhappy years with an uncongenial stepmother, Father decided to run away from home. He packed a few clothes and a small supply of food in a flour sack, improvised a crude raft and, accompanied by a younger sister, floated down the Mississippi river. Two days later, hungry and tired, they beached their log raft near a beautiful colonial mansion which seemed to promise refuge. Thus Father began a life of adventure—a saga which would take him to some of the farthest reaches of the universe."

"The owner of the beautiful mansion took them in, these two nondescript children, who refused to divulge their parents names, and adopted them. Thus, reared and educated in a perfect environment, they probably had a better start in life had they not run away! Father was a brilliant youngster, of dashing and distinguished appearance, who grew rapidly into a handsome, well-turned-out man. In 1840, at the age of 22, he became a government surveyor. Nine years later (1849), he went to California in the gold rush. He struck it rich and in 1851, tiring of the hectic and dangerous life of the gold fields, returned to Mississippi. Twice on this journey he was robbed by Mexican and Indian Outlaws—once at Eagle Pass and later near San Antonio. He was unharmed both times and, sensibly, had sent his gold ahead of him by safer means."

"When the Civil War began in 1861, he enlisted as a soldier in the Confederate Army but during the training period he injured a foot so severely that he gave up the idea of active service. He secured a transfer to the Intelligence Department and in the capacity of a spy, carried on for the South. He never told me of any tight spots or exciting adventures experienced during his secret service work. After the war he could not settle down to the routine life on a plantation. Thus began a five year search for new places to live, experienced more wealth. He never told much more than that (about) his travels except that he crossed the Atlantic twice and the Pacific three times. He did say, I remember, that he tried a venture in South America which was wrecked by a yellow fever epidemic. He fled on a ship and the whole crew—there were no passengers – of 19 men succumbed to Cholera. He credited his living to taking massive doses of calomel, a procedure at which the unfortunate victims laughed. I shall never forget his cryptic account of the latter stages of the disaster. 'I', he said sardonically, 'had to throw the last man overboard'. Many of them died, he continued, 'I threw them all gone. I did, I performed for each one a Ritual before dumping him into the sea. When the last had gone, the ship was becalmed. For three months I lived on that ship, lonelier and more forlorn that I have ever been before. Even when the rescuers came, I was

### beforeiremember_p2 — Before I Remember
kept abord, towed to port, and quarantined. Most of my gray hairs can be traced to that experience, he concluded."

"Upon his release, he went to Texas and claimed two thousand acres of land in North Texas. In an area located about 16 miles east of Sherman, he laid out a town in his honor. In that same year – 1874 – he married my Mother, Mrs Julia E. Davis, who many years later, told me about the prominence he enjoyed in this section of Texas. She said after their wedding and spending their honeymoon in Denison, they were welcomed back to Savoy by a band and practically the whole town."

"But marriage and five children by him and three she had by Dr. William Davis were not enough to keep him settled. In 1886 he went to New Mexico, bought an interest in a silver mine and stayed for so long that Mother feared that some tragedy had befallen him. Two years later he reappeared, a broken man. There had been some tragic occurrence about which he was silent. No one ever knew what it could have been."

"His fortune was dissipated, it was apparent. A few months after his return from New Mexico, he went to West Texas and homesteaded 2300 acres near Eastland. He also claimed 640 acres in what is now the Ranger Oil Field. I won't allow myself to think of how rich we would have become if he had been able to hold onto the Ranger claim. Due to back taxes, a faulty deed, prior claims, he eventually lost all of his holdings in this area. He never gave up. When he died 1889 at Joshua in Johnson County, Texas, of cancer of the stomach, he was fighting to recoup his lost wealth. Investigations after his death revealed that he was practically destitute. By that time we were old enough to carry on ourselves but for myself I would have almost preferred to know about all of his adventures than to have been left a small fortune!"

That is Mother's history of her father. Now in similar fashion, she told me what she knew about Robert A. Travis, my Father: "According to the accounts given to me by W. A. Caldwell, the son of one of the members of a family who knew your Father's family well, three Travis brothers came to the United States from England in the latter part of the 18th century. They landed in New York and during the ensuing years moved south and westward. Your Great Grandfather settled in the wilds of Tennessee Territory near Nashville where your Grandfather, William Travis, was born."

"In the year 1848 this William Travis, grown and married, migrated to central Mississippi and acquired a 640-acre section of land 6 ½ miles west of Canton, in Madison County. The Caldwells moved at the same time and claimed lands adjoining your Grandfather's. They owned slaves and cultivated their lands intensively and within a few years became prosperous. Their holdings became 'plantations' and they enjoyed life with slaves to wait on them, and by social activities with similar neighbors who have developed plantations. In the year 1851, plus a few years, your Father, Robert Anderson Travis, was born your Grandfather's plantation was moving along smoothly. He managed his slaves kindly and efficiently, he was well liked by his neighbors and was just getting everything running well when in the summer of 1854 he died of typhoid fever. Eight days later his wife succumbed to the same disease. Their tragic deaths – he

### beforeiremember_p3 — Before I Remember
was 44 and his wife, Martha, was a few years younger – left your Father, the youngest, and four other children orphaned. Fortunately Jim Caldwell and his sister Jane Caldwell, two refined people, took charge of the plantation and saw to it that these children were reared and educated properly."

"Your Father's education and growth to manhood were typical of that period of southern living. The plantation was well supplied with slaves and they did all of the tasks: tilled the soil, gathered and stored the crops, did all domestic duties in and around the 'Big House,' the owner's home. The plantation owner rode a fine horse over the 'place' to oversee the activities of his slaves, or had an overseer do this. Thus it was easy and natural for them to live lives of aristocrats and pursue whatever activities which struck their fancy. Your Father attended school, Coats Academy along with the children of other plantation owners. He grew up to enjoy fine picnics, dances, parties which they attended in styles and fashions of the era. Festivities were arranged so old and young could take part at the same time. It was very proper to go to Church on a regular and conscientious basis – both on Sundays and on Wednesdays to prayer meetings."

"In 1861 the Civil War began but from what I could gather it did not affect his mode of existence appreciably. While many of the neighbors took part in the war, and some of the major action took place in Mississippi, notably at Vicksburg, and he was ten years old when it started luckily he and the Caldwells escaped harm. While the result of the war was that the slaves were freed, most of them elected to stay on the plantation and either worked for wages or on 'shares.'"

"So, Mr. Robert Travis continued to grow to manhood as a Southern Aristocrat. He rode fine horses, acted some as 'Overseer' as he got older, and led an active social life with the other young aristocrats. In his early 30's he fell desperately in love with a Miss Atworth. Her Father refused to allow her to marry him on the grounds that he looted too much and drank a bit too much instead of changing his mode of living, he persisted in living the way he preferred and lost her. He and his gang kept up their way of life and, eventually, he got over her."

"As soon as he had grown up enough he took charge of the plantation, so his social life didn't keep him from being a success at that. The Negroes liked Mr. 'Bob' Travis very much. He was willing to work hard for them. I used to marvel at his ability to get along with them so well and some of the slaves used to regale me with stories of the kind and thoughtful things he had done for them when they needed help."

"When he was 35 years old he married a Miss Ozzie Bilbo, a part Choctaw Indian. Miss Bilbo lived in Yazoo County, which adjoined Madison County, and while visiting in Canton, your Father met her. She was a very attractive young lady and created a sensation among the eligible young men there. Your Father was the lucky one. In the Spring of 1897 he moved his family to Caddo, Indian Territory. It seems that after his marriage, Charles Bilbo, his wife's brother, had gone to Indian Territory to claim lands granted to members of the Five Civilized Tribes – Chickasaw, Choctaw, Cherokee, Creek and Seminole – and your Father's move was to claim lands for Ozzie and their children: Robbie, Marie and William. He kept his Mississippi properties but decided it best to live in Indian Territory on the lands they had claimed successfully. Just as everything

### beforeiremember_p4 — Before I Remember
was really getting organized and running smoothly, his wife died in 1898. This left him with Robbie, Marie and Will to rear. Robbie, the older girl, was no problem, for she wanted a formal education which suited him fine. Marie wanted a Business Training Course which he deplored, for in those days, young ladies didn't go into business! William just wanted to be a cowboy, which your Father deplored too. Mrs. Bilbo, the mother-in-law, sided with the children and did much to alienate them from your Father. She continued to meddle after your Father and I were married, but I will go into that later."

Here is Mother's story of her early life: "I was born in Savoy, Texas, on November 26th, 1874, the third of four children born to Julia E. Davis Savoy and Colonel William Louis Marshall Savoy. My Mother had had three children by her first husband, Dr. Davis, prior to his death. There were Ernest, Irene and Emma Marshall, Joe and Ira. We grew up and were educated in Savoy. We were a closely knit family unit and our education, play, courtships, trials and tribulations were shared by all members of the family, and even to some extent by the inhabitants of this small, warm settlement. One of my most treasured memories is of my years attending 'Savoy Male and Female College,' which was conducted by and administered by Professor R. R. Halsel, a brilliant and scholarly man, to whom all who attended it owe a tremendous debt of gratitude. Young people today might consider it primitive but to me it still symbolizes the ultimate in educational institutions. Many of his graduates went on to make history in Texas and National affairs. He was brilliant in book knowledge tu his outstanding characteristic was to inspire his students with lasting enthusiasms. Though I was handicapped by poor eyesight, even at times being unable to read all of my assignments, he inspired me to attain a more thorough education than any other teacher of mine, at any stage of my formal education. In later years it was a joy to go back to Savoy College for the reunions and to recall those glorious days with other graduates!"

"Of my brothers and half-sisters and half-brothers, none ever became famous or accomplished any outstanding record. As brilliant and accomplished and distinguished as my father was, he failed to pass these qualities on to us! The most promising of us was Ira. He was a dreamer and most resembled his Father. I remember his jumping up in the middle of the night to dash off some poetry, some of it exquisite. I kept much of it for many years but unfortunately lost it somewhere. But in 1898, when very young, he joined the army because of the Spanish American War and died of pneumonia in San Francisco, and was buried there in the Presidio. I have visited his grave and became very saddened while viewing it because he was my favorite and seemed to have such brilliant promise."

"Our social and other activities in those days would be considered tame to youth of 1935, but we had fun. We, of course, didn't have automobiles and radios and the other paraphernalia of today but we did many things in a quiet way and as I look back on it, we didn't miss out on so many tricks. There were dances, picnics, school parties, church socials. Going to church and to prayer meetings and Sunday School were an important part of our schedule. We had boyfriends and managed to do quite a bit of 'courting.' I was pretty, in the middle of everything, always felt keenly in love with some young man, who loved me equally, and one time we had a fight and before we 'made up' he died suddenly. It took me quite a while to get over this."

### beforeiremember_p5 — Before I Remember
"Father's death in 1889 disrupted our lives economically, more than emotionally, because he was away from home so much that he was almost a stranger to use, he had always made lots of money. When it was discovered at his death that he was practically destitute, we suddenly found the need to get busy and provide a livelihood ourselves. The boys were old enough to get out on their own. Irene, my half-sister, married a Mr. Dodd, who owned a drug store in Caddo and moved there to live. I stayed at home to help Mother. In 1897 I married J. B. Rogers, Jr., and we lived in Farmersville for a year. Rogers was a craftsman in appieance, distantly related to William Rogers, the silverware manufacturer, and an expert watchmaker and repairer. He was a sick man, suffering from catarrhal tuberculosis when we married, and he also had a drinking problem. His drinking undoubtedly hastened his death in 1902, after a prolonged period of illness. We were very happy while he lived and it was not a wasted or barren experience. Looking back on him after his death, I realized that I had married him out of sympathy. Mother was living with us at the time of his death and we stayed in Farmersville for the next few years. Mother taught a small group of people pupils of grammar school age and also had a number of guitar students, but it was a precarious way to make a living in such a small town."

"Mr. Travis, your Father, came into the picture in 1904 while I was on a visit in Caddo to see Irene. How did I meet him? He told me after we were married that he had seen me walking on the street there and without a moment's hesitation said to himself, 'There is the woman for me!' However, we were not introduced until several months later because I left Caddo. Caddo was a small town and it wasn't difficult to find out who I was so on my next visit there, he found me!"

At this point in Mother's narrative I asked her, 'Had you noticed him that first time on the street in Caddo?'

'Yes.'

'Did you think, 'There is the man for me'?'

'Of course not! I did notice him though because he was a very attractive man.'

"We began to see each other more frequently while I was in Caddo and corresponded after I went back to Farmersville. That we didn't write just about the weather, we got married on September 26 – My 29th birthday – 1905 in Sherman, Texas. So he kept his promise to himself, but I won't deny that I had 'something to do with it'!"

"After a short honeymoon, we lived in Caddo with Robbie, Marie, William and Mrs. Bilbo – 3 stepchildren and an obnoxious mother-in-law! Robbie married soon after to Will McBride, a nice man, and left, in a short time trouble really began, because Mrs. Bilbo had created such a hostile atmosphere that it soon became unbearable. Mr. Travis was busy filing the land claims so he was not aware of the seriousness of the situation. I tried everything within reason to effect it happily but nothing worked. Meanwhile, you were about to be born so I just went along from day to day until your birth on October 5, 1906, at Caddo. You were a fine, lusty boy! After regaining

### beforeiremember_p6 — Before I Remember
my strength I decided that the situation was unbearable. I couldn't get your Father to face up to the problem, so I decided when you were 7 months old to move to Douglas, Arizona. I was heart-broken but you were such a healthy, energetic young ruffian that I got strength from you! Uncles Marshall and Joe were living in Douglas and using Mexicans chiefly to did all the stores and stores. Though surprised to see me – I hadn't given them much warning – they welcomed me with open arms, which I needed. Douglas was a typical bordertown of that era, rough, ready, but they were able to take care of themselves and us. After a few months, I became more reconciled to the situation, grew stronger physically and psychologically, in the bracing Arizona climate. You continued to be healthy and vigorous. Uncle Marshall bought us a bicycle and put a basket on it, so I would take us for bicycle rides around Douglas and over the border into Agua Prieta, a small Mexican village and safe enough during the daytime.' (Years later I went to Douglas in the summer and walked across the border, just to get a picture of what it could have looked like in 1907.)

"It was fun riding the bicycle, you liked the view from your basket, and it was excellent exercise for me. In the meantime I corresponded with your Father, taking the position that what he did was up to him, but that I would not come back to Caddo. Finally, he decided that you and I were more important to him than were Mrs. Bilbo and the plantation. He came to Douglas to live. He was a different person there, it was had fitting into the wild and wooly atmosphere! it proved to be more than a Southern Gentleman could bear, so we went back to Caddo to take care of some business affairs and then onto the plantation near Virilia, Mississippi, in the Spring of 1907."

"It was a dreary situation, with rain, muddy roads, many Negroes around, rattling around in a large plantation home, 6 ½ miles out from Canton, a place, nice enough itself, of about 3000 people estimated, far from Arizona with its dry, though warm climate, it was understandable that I was disappointed. I was more excited about this than Mr. Travis. The minute he got back he was a 'new man.' His whole being seemed to say, 'I am home, at last!' He went to work with a vengeance, painting and renovating the plantation house in which we lived. The Negroes called it the 'Big House.' It became a glistening white Colonial home, on a small scale. The Negro quarters were renovated, the land cultivated and the cotton and corn planted. The Negroes seemed to worship him, so they worked with enthusiasm. In late Spring, we had more visitors than I can count, began to see how he could be so enthusiastic about it. It became a drowsy paradise. Life on this place became a perfect experience and I look back on the years from 1907 to 1912 as being the happiest of my life. Marie and William continued to live in Oklahoma (It became a state in 1907) so they were no problem. On January 2, 1910, I gave birth to Lee and the birth was a hard one, due to the ineptness of a country doctor and the fact that I was weakened by malaria. But Lee was OK and I came out of it in good shape."

"As I became more and more accustomed to 'Southern Living,' with its quiet and lazy dignity, the more I savored it. I began to wonder if the wildness and primitive life in Douglas was so 'ideal' after all. We had Negros to do 'everything'; cook, wash, farm, whatever and they were happy. I was happy for many years they had been over for many years and they were 'liberated.' They weren't slaves officially, but in reality, they lived the same as they did as slaves. We went into Canton in a Surrey, had good horses

to ride over the place, for Mr. Travis to 'oversee' and for me to ride for pleasure. As you grew up, you and Lee romped all over the place. With servants in the house to cook and bathe you and many times I yelled myself hoarse trying to get you to come home from the Negro quarters, or to stop throwing dirt or dust on a wretched pickaninny, or to get you out of the rain, or to stop doing other things that two white ruffianly-inclined brats could find to do on a 640-acre plantation. One time you ran away after some fancied wrong and some Negro brought you in from near the Virilia Store where you had lost yourself. You were pretty terrified by this experience and I don't remember you ever 'running away' again. I wrote your Father for not taking your disappearance more seriously, but he said, 'Nothing could have happened to the little rascal!' and left it at that. 'We had both healthy youngsters but you did catch some of the children's diseases: measles and whooping cough and some others but recovered without any damage. When you were ill, it kept me quite busy and I marveled how a Mother could take care of 5 or 10 children as many of the Negro parents had.'

"Aside from just playing with the Negroes, they were an important influence in your early life. With servants in the house to cook, make up beds, clean and bathe you, to call you 'Big Boss' and Lee 'Little Boss.' We had several servants in the house over the early years. Then Aunt Ellie Cross came and she was a remarkable person in every way. We first hired her in a relief role and she was obviously just what we needed! She, her husband and one son lived on the place and were share-croppers. She became a member of the family. She was in her sixties, small, bustlingly energetic, clean, efficient, kindly disposed and so 'worldly-wise'! But she was a scrapper too, and I've seen her rear up and fight like a tigress when the situation demanded it! She was an important influence in your lives and deserves much credit for your turning out to be upstanding sons! She stayed on with us after Mr. Travis died and was a great help to me in keeping the farm going. It is difficult for me to find words to express fully my high regard and appreciation for this remarkable woman!"

"There were Negro men too who cannot be forgotten. Several of them lived on the place and worked for Mr. Travis for years, farming by either renting, share-cropping or for wages. The ones working for various usages used their time drawing water, cutting wood, drawing water from our well, gathering corn, mowing and stacking or baling hay, repairing fences, digging ditches, maintaining roads. The renters paid rent for their quarters and their share of the crop from the farm, at the near Virilia, for example. The share croppers lived in the quarters, had their own mules and plow and their crop was with us. The 'Quarters' was a group of cabins located close to each other and most of the families had many children, with pickaninies overrunning the area. Much of the time you played with these children and when you did get up with them, it was hard to tell you and Lee from them."

"One of the leading Negroes on the place was Marlen 'Man' Dyson. He was louttish, easy going, with a family of eight or nine children by his own wife and probably some by other Negroes' wives! He was a sanctimonious profligate and was always to be found on the mourner's bench at Mount Hope Church. He was really 'good at huntin' after Mr. Travis' death, I relied on him very much. Samson Diamond was another, of an entirely different type. The other Negroes called him a 'smart' Negro. He was a hard worker, good at operating machinery and handling jobs that required

intelligence and versatility. Though I was never able to catch him, I am convinced that he was a clever and resourceful thief. I finally had to run him off, which I should have done long before for he really began to filch more after Mr. Travis' death."

"There were many others, of various types and personalities, but 'Mon' and Samson are good examples. With this many of them living so close together, they would get into cutting or shooting scrapes and on numerous occasions, at various hours of the night, usually, there would be a knock on our door and a muffled voice saying, 'Boss! Boss!' Mr. Travis would go to the door and invariably he was asked to call a doctor to come take care of the 'victims.' One shot Negro was near death, Mr. Travis told me. His wife found Tom with Liza Williams together and cut him up pretty badly about the throat and chest and he has little chance of recovery.' Many of them carried razors or knives with them and quite a few bore scars evidencing that they had participated in cuttings."

"Much has been said and written about the Negro louttishness, and aimlessness and they are this way to a degree, but much of it is due to the setup in which they find themselves. The share croppers are usually industrious because the more they grow, the more they make. After Mr. Travis' death – and I'll tell you about that later – I was suddenly thrown in full charge of the place, so I really got an education in The Black Person. So, to a degree, I agree with a story told by a Southern writer who said that one day he came upon a Negro youth sitting on a fence and asked him what he did for a living. 'Nothing,' he replied, 'I lives with my father.' 'Well,' continued the writer, 'What does your father do for a living?' 'Nothing,' replied the youth."

"One of the clearest things I remember about you was the difficulty I had getting you to walk the first time. You were eighteen months old before we could get you to even make any effort to even stand upright. I was most worried about this than Mr. Travis. Since you had no visible impairments, he felt that in good time you'd just stand up and get going! You kept putting it off and got very proficient in scooting around on the floor in a inimitable style which was a marvel of locomotion! This not only got you from place to place, but it enabled you to wear out the seats of your dresses[?] regularly. You refused to walk – until one day I decided that maybe a good and firm whipping would motivate you. 'Young man,' I said very firmly while brandishing a large and sinister switch, 'If you don't get up and walk this instant, I am going to whip you long and severely!' Whereupon, you got up and walked away from me, without a word!"

"Another happening I remember is that you and Lee were playing in a field and I called for you to come home. You decided to jump over a fence instead of going around as you usually did. You got over it all right, but Lee got caught in the barbed wire and began to yell and scream at the top of his voice. You were about 150 yards away and I must have covered the distance in 10 seconds! You and me were trying desperately but ineffectually to get him down, and the more we pulled at the barbs, the barbs scratched him, so the louder he yelled. We were able, or course, to release him, but even after he was on the ground, he continued to cry – much of it from being terrified at the situation. This taught you and him a lesson, and I don't ever remember your crawling through or over a barb wire fence after that."

"When you were about 3 ½ years old Marie and Robbie came to visit us. You and Lee got sick of the same time and when they arrived you were both in bed. I don't think that you got sick deliberately, but when they found out you were under the weather, they proceeded to buy candy, toys, clothes – just about everything you 'brats' could want! Before you were able to get out of bed – and this is the reason I think your illnesses were feigned – you made them promise to send you a train and some automobiles. Marie and Robbie were obviously enjoying watching you enjoy the gifts. They must have spent $50.00! Incidentally, after your Father left them in Caddo, they became 'friendly' and got back on good terms with him, though they continued to live in Oklahoma."

"Now as I look back on those years from the time we moved to Virilia from Douglas until the middle of April, 1912, were the happiest days of our lives. It was at that time that he told me casually, 'I have a peculiar swelling – a knot-like growth – in my throat. It is not particularly painful, but it seems to be getting larger.' I felt it and advised him to see a doctor soon. The next week he saw a doctor in Canton, our family physician, who didn't seem to think it was serious, and would go away in time. With that he decided that it would heal itself. However this was not the case, so we went to see Dr. Roan, a cancer specialist in Wesson, Mississippi, who diagnosed it as cancer and said that it had been ignored much too long. He said that Mr. Travis should go to Kellam Hospital in Richmond, VA, for immediate expert treatment. I still remember how white and shaken your Father was when he reported this to us. Those ten months in Kellam Hospital must have been the most miserable of his life. We couldn't be with him for I had to oversee the place and you and Lee were getting to an age where you, Ira, and Lee very soon afterward, had to be in school."

"He wrote regularly and his letters were always optimistic, even though I learned later that he knew very early that he didn't have a chance of surviving. In September he wrote, 'I am getting better and the doctors think I can be released in a couple of weeks.' His cheerfulness is what kept me going: I ran the place, managed the Negroes, borrowed money from the bank at his behest to meet all expenses, confident that he would be back with us any week to take charge. Harvest time rolled around, winter came, and he was in the beautiful the snow was in Richmond, White and Ira and I began to doubt his optimism. He died June 25, 1913, in the hospital and was buried in the Family Graveyard located about a mile north of the Big House."

"You begin to remember now, don't you, some of these things?"

"Yes," I replied. This was true, but some of my memories were, then, hazy. I was approaching 7O years of age and from then on I do remember more clearly, so I'll start the next Chapter – I Begin to Remember.

ANNUAL SAVOY REUNION HELD JUNE 18, 1967

Mrs. Alpha Jenkins

The annual reunion of ex-students and descendants of Prof. R. R. Halsell's Savoy Male and Female College was held Sunday, June 18, 1967, in the new Savoy school auditorium with 31 in attendance. Only one ex-student was able to be present, Mrs. Alpha Deatherage Jenkins of Savoy. In spite of the fact that students are near 90 years of age and unable to attend, their descendants continued to meet each third Sunday in June to perpetuate the memory of Prof. Halsell and his school. The faculty and pupils of the present Savoy school made us feel very welcome for the rooms had been decorated with posters and pictures and signs of "Welcome Halsell Exes and Friends" and "Glad To See You Again!" One exhibit of interest to all was an old copy of The Platonism, the school paper ad stating that the Halsell College was established in 1876 and chartered in 1879. Professor Halsell was president and professor of mathematics and rhetoric; Lewis Holland, vice-president was professor of Latin, Greek, Mental Science; L. W. Pierce, A. B., professor of Natural Science, and L. J. Bowden was professor of Business/Penmanship. The first reunion was held in 1938, forty-eight years after the school had burned in 1890.

Sunday's reunion was opened by the president, Kenneth Woodward of Duncan, Okla., and a prayer by Glenmore Hodge of Bonham. Each one present introduced himself and among the group there were 13 children of ex-students and 3 grandchildren.

Mrs. G. R. Newby, secretary, read letters from many who could not be present and gave a treasurers' report.

Mrs. Glenmore Hodge led in several hymn sings accompanied by Mr. Kenneth Woodward at the piano.

Mr. Woodward read a poem, "Savoy University," composed and printed by an ex-student, Lew Reynolds of Dallas.

Mrs. Hodge called on all to "Pray," accompanied by Mrs. Woodward.

Ken Woodward played "Yesterday" on the guitar.

A quartet composed of Mrs. Ernest Ayres and Mrs. Doyle Ernest and Earle Ayres, sang "Have Thine Own Way, Lord."

The noon meal was served by Mrs. Frances Cates in the lunch room, compliments of the Ex-Students Association.

In the afternoon session the school superintendent, Mr. Robert Hodges, spoke briefly and in behalf of the school thanked Mrs. Barbara Woodward Lips, granddaughter of Prof. Halsell, for the new Steinway piano she had given Savoy school in honor of her mother, Mrs. Annie Halsell Woodward of Durant, Okla., who recently passed away.

"How Greet Thou Art" was sung by Mrs. Glenmore Hodge followed by an address by Mrs. Lips, a beautiful tribute to the memory of her grandfather, Prof. Halsell.

The same officers were re-elected for another year: Kenneth Woodward, president; Glenmore Hodge, vice-president; and Mrs. G. R. Newby, secretary-treasurer.

The day closed with singing "Til We Meet" and a prayer by G. R. Woodward.

Those registering were Mrs. Zola Layman of Sherman; Mrs. Alpha Jenkins of Savoy and her daughter, Mamie Joe Jenkins of Dallas; Elizabeth Large, Mrs. S. G. Elmo; Gene Blakey, Mr. and Mrs. Lhode Stephenson; Mr. and Mrs. Robert Hodges, Mr. and Mrs. Kenneth Woodward of Savoy; Mr. and Mrs. Kenneth Woodward and son Ken of Duncan, Okla.; Mrs. Glenmore H. A TRIBUTE TO
GRANDFATHER HALSELL
by Mrs. Charles S. Lips

I am grateful for the privilege to be here today and take part in this reunion, particularly since it is honoring the memory of my grandfather. Professor Halsell. A personality that arrested and demanded consideration. One look into the depths of those blue eyes made you feel it, greatness, a power, and man. A noble character, a character that will live in the knowledge of Infinite Being.

For through his example I have understood what it means to make your life a pattern, rather than to seek to make a place in life through other means. I never think of my grandfather without thinking of the 13th Chapter of I Corinthians, because it held under his very special meaning for those whose life is guided by and given to the love and consideration for others. THE ONLY VALUL of A LIFE IS ITS CONTENT— FOR "OTHERS."

He built a reputation second to none in his profession. For him all the knowledge and wisdom he possessed was worthless unless it was passed on to others. It was a great influence of love and courage for the youth of his time. It caused him to establish and guide the great institution of Savoy College — such an institution, with what other purpose could there be for such an advantage? Yet "The task is never as great before you as the Power is behind you." He did not delay because he knew his duty, your reward, your destiny— are here and now. Seemingly, he gave everything he had — his money, his time, and his energy to make a place where youth could gain the knowledge they wanted despite their economic circumstances — every boy and girl who came to him asking for an education, he gave them that chance. A man with the rare gift of kindling to a consuming flame the very faintest kind of a spark of celestial fire that might be smoldering in the soul of a student. He knew his students could not do it alone, only God, but rejoiced if God found a use for his efforts because great love is unconscious of itself.

He had this burning desire that his students have the right relationship with their God. He always uttered a prayer and it is significant to me that this was done that each thing was of God because "For I met God in the morning, when my day was at its best, and His Presence had seemed to rise like a glory in the breast, all day long the Presence lingered, all day long He stayed with me, and we sailed in perfect calmness over a very troubled sea." I don't know all the reason, learned from many a troubled way; you must see Him in His light if you want to walk through the day.

He was a great disseminator. It is obedience, not comprehension he taught, that enables a person to act wisely. "He came to us as one unknown without a name, as of old by the lake side He came to those men who knew Him not. I speaks to us the same we: "Follow Me" — He commands. And to those who obey Him whether they be wise or simple He reveals Himself, step by step, as an ineffable mystery they shall learn in their own experience. Who He is."

I am certain today that had my grandfather lived to see it he would most assuredly have been embarrassed by the things that are being said; but now because Grandfather possessed the great quality of Humility— to have humility is to experience reality not in relation to ourselves, but in its sacred independence. I am sure he saw in his investment a crop ripe for harvest — not to be stored in barns but to be given to the land to bring forth fruits in another life, or place or time. You can be sure that what he sowed and the yield that has come from your growth has caused his Internal joy and more than the trial was thought possible so many years ago. Through you he has been placed in a "Hall of Fame" within my heart, as perhaps in the hearts of others, it becomes one why I have felt compelled to carry on this reunion each year to honor his memory and to remind the influence meant in your lives . . . and to remind the world that real wealth is not measured by the dollar mark, but that true worth is measured all the time by the moral harvest through love in the lives of those with whom you were in contact during your sojourn here on earth. All his faith would have been for nothing without this great love. Through his love he was able to lead others and set their feet and hands to the great dreams of his life.

He has left an indelible imprint upon the lives and characters of countless ones who sat at his feet gleaning from his mind the knowledge of living. Thank you for the love and kindness to my grandfather's fond memory. We will always remember with love and devotion the things you have done to perpetuate his memory and enliven his idea. Professor Halsell walks the grounds around here no more—

DEATH ENDS CAREER OF W. E. McMAHON

W. E. McMahon

Judge William Emmet McMahon, 90, a former teacher and judge, attorney for Standard Oil of New Jersey, veteran of World War I and an attorney, died in a Bonham rest home at 9:15 a.m. Saturday, April 1, 1967. Funeral services were held at the Wise Funeral Chapel followed by graveside services at Restland in Dallas.

Judge McMahon was a native of Fannie county, Texas, was born in Savoy on March 23, 1876, the son of Mr. and Mrs. David McMahon,

pioneer residents of Fannin county.

He attended Halsell college at Savoy and was one of the last surviving students of that school.

Judge McMahon attended the University of Texas and after graduating from that school in law began a career that took him into many parts of the world.

After graduating from the University of Texas, he returned to Bonham where he engaged in the private practice of law for several years.

He then went to the Philippine Islands as a school teacher but gave it up and returned to learn the law. It was while engaged in the Philippines that he was appointed to the office of federal district judge in the Philippines, a position he held until his return to the United States in 1915.

He entered the advocate general's department of the army and served until the end of War I.

Judge McMahon then practiced law in Tampico, Mexico, and then became vice president of one of the legal department of the Standard Oil Company of New Jersey with headquarters in Mexico. He remained in Mexico until 1938.

He returned to Texas in 1938 and lived in Dallas before returning to Bonham in the 1940s to make his home.

Judge McMahon was first married to Miss Sally Burnett of Bonham. His children, Mrs. Jean Scarborough Dritt who preceded him in death.

He is survived by his two sons, David McMahon and Bob McMahon of Fort Worth, and a step-son Billy Burnett, and two sisters, Mrs. Minnie Pierce of Savoy and Mrs. Florine Stubbs of Fort Worth, and several grandchildren and nieces and nephews.
